Dark Trap vs Drill: Sound, Songwriting & 808s

Dark trap and drill get lumped together a lot. Both are heavy, both use 808s, and both live in a minor key. But they're built differently, they feel different, and they ask different things of the artist. This guide covers the real differences between dark trap and drill, how the 808s and beats work in each, how to write songs and hooks in dark trap, and some mixing tips for 808 slides.

What is dark trap?

Trap came out of the southern United States, especially Atlanta, in the 2000s: rolling hi-hats, booming 808 bass, and snares or claps on the third beat of each bar. Dark trap takes that skeleton and pushes the mood. Think horror-movie melodies, distorted bass, reverb-soaked vocals, and an atmosphere that feels more like a soundtrack than a club record.

Dark trap is less about bounce and more about mood. The beat is often slow and spacious, and the vocal can be rapped, half-sung or drenched in effects.

What is drill?

Drill started in Chicago and was rebuilt in London, and that UK version is the template most drill uses today, including in Toronto. Its signatures are sliding 808 basslines, off-beat syncopated snares, and fast triplet hi-hats, usually around 140 BPM in half-time. Drill is tense and driving. It pushes forward. For the full background, see Toronto Drill Music: The Complete Guide.

The key differences

Dark trap Drill
Drums Snare or clap on beat 3 (half-time), rolling hats Syncopated, off-beat snares; triplet hat rolls
808s Long, sustained, often distorted Short to medium, sliding between notes
Tempo feel Slow, spacious Driving, bouncy
Melody Cinematic, eerie, layered Sparse, cold, repetitive
Vocals Rapped, sung or effected; lots of space Cadence-driven rapping; tight to the drums
Mood Atmospheric, brooding Tense, confrontational

The simplest way to hear it: tap along to the snare. If it lands steadily on the same spot every bar, you're probably in trap. If it skips around and lands in unexpected places, you're in drill.

Where they meet

A lot of current music mixes the two: drill drums and slides under dark trap's cinematic melodies and sung hooks. That blend lets an artist keep drill's energy in the verses and open up into something bigger on the hook.

808 patterns: dark trap vs drill

In dark trap, the 808 often holds long notes, sometimes following the root of the chord and sometimes hitting a single note under a whole section. Distortion or saturation is common so the bass is audible on small speakers. Pitch glides are used, but as accents.

In drill, the 808 is closer to a lead instrument. It slides between notes constantly, and those slides often carry the hook of the beat. Patterns are rhythmic and leave space for the snares.

Practical tips for writing 808 patterns: - Tune your 808 to the key of the track. An out-of-key 808 is the most common reason a beat sounds wrong. - Use glide (portamento) on overlapping notes to get slides. Keep slides short in dark trap and more frequent in drill. - Don't let the 808 and kick fight. Either layer a kick that's tuned to the 808 or let the 808 do the work alone. - Leave gaps. Silence before a slide makes it hit harder.

Mixing 808 slides

  • Check in mono. Low end should be centred. Stereo bass falls apart on club systems and phone speakers.
  • Saturate for translation. Gentle distortion adds harmonics so the bass can be heard on earbuds without turning it up.
  • Sidechain lightly. Duck the 808 a little under the kick if they overlap.
  • Watch the slides. Slides can create sudden level jumps. Automate volume or use a compressor so they stay even.
  • Reference. Compare your mix to commercial tracks you like at the same volume.

Dark trap songwriting and hooks

Dark trap gives a writer more room than drill does. The beats are slower and more open, so there's space for melody, longer phrases and mood.

Start with the feeling. Decide what the song is about emotionally before you write a bar: paranoia, loss, revenge, loneliness, ambition.

Write the hook first. In dark trap the hook carries the song. Keep it short (two to four lines), repeatable, and melodic. Hum melodies over the beat before you put words to them.

Use contrast. A rapped verse into a sung hook is one of the most effective moves in the style. It's also what makes the drill/dark trap blend work: aggressive verse, open hook.

Use images, not explanations. "Shadows on the ceiling, phone face down" says more than "I'm stressed."

Leave space. Not every gap needs a word. Ad-libs and silence are part of the arrangement.

Dark trap in Toronto

Toronto has a long history with dark, moody rap and R&B. The city's sound has been known for years for atmosphere, minor keys and space, and that lines up naturally with dark trap. Mixing it with the city's drill scene was a short step. For more on the city's sound, read Toronto Rap Scene 2026.

FAQ

Is dark trap the same as drill? No. Dark trap uses trap drums with a darker, cinematic mood. Drill uses syncopated snares and sliding 808s with a more driving feel.

What BPM is dark trap? It varies, but many dark trap beats are around 130 to 160 BPM in half-time, so they feel slow.

Can a song be both? Yes. Drill drums with dark trap melodies and sung hooks is a common blend.
