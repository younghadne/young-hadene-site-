Nightmares vs In the Nighttime: Two Young Hadene Videos Compared

Published October 22, 2026 · Music · By Young Hadene Team

Both titles suggest the dark. They went up on Young Hadene's YouTube channel on the same day, May 20, 2026, and each lasts under two minutes. That's about where the similarities end. One was filmed under grey winter skies among bare trees. The other, despite being called "In the Nighttime", was shot in bright sunshine.

We compared the two uploads side by side, first on what you can see and then on what you can hear. Everything here comes from the videos themselves, their YouTube listings and Apple Music's public catalogue. We don't quote lyrics: there's no official lyric sheet, and auto-captions aren't accurate enough to quote.

The picture

Nightmares: winter woods and quiet streets

The video starts with the word "Nightmares" on a black screen. For a couple of seconds the title sits over a forest shot, with "str8hitsrecords" written across it. Around 0:04 the camera tilts up into a canopy of bare branches. That sets the palette for the rest: leafless trees, a forest floor covered in brown leaves, and a sky that changes from flat grey to patches of blue.

The video moves between those woods and a residential street with detached houses, driveways and wooden fences. Young Hadene wears a black puffer jacket throughout, with sunglasses in many shots. The camera usually stays at arm's length and is often handheld. The last stretch, from about 1:32, cuts down to his feet and jeans moving through dead leaves. The video keeps going for about ten seconds after the song ends.

In the Nighttime: daylight, concrete and grass

This one has no title card. It starts in a parking-garage setting with concrete walls and pillars, then moves outside into hard, low winter sunlight. There's a strong lens flare around 0:28. The locations include low-rise apartment buildings, a red-brick block, a parking lot with cars, and open lawns of yellow-green grass under bare trees. Here he wears a camouflage-pattern puffer over a hoodie, with the hood up in some shots.

The tone is lighter than in "Nightmares". Around 1:04 he moves across a lawn, and at 1:12 there's a wide smile straight into the lens. The final frame, at about 1:16, is an apartment building against clear blue sky.

The title clash. Nothing in the frames we pulled is actually shot at night. Everything we saw is in daylight. A title doesn't have to describe its footage, and the gap may be deliberate. We'd rather ask than guess.

The sound

We scanned each audio track in three-second windows, measuring overall level and how much of the energy sits in the low bass (below about 120 Hz, where 808s and kick drums live). Auto-captions told us when a voice is present. The results map the structure of each song without our needing to transcribe a word.

Nightmares

  • 0:00–0:26: A quieter opening, roughly 7–8 dB below the main body of the song, with almost no low bass. Voice is already present from about 0:05, so the song opens with vocals over a stripped-back beat, not an instrumental intro.
  • About 0:27: The low end comes in, and the level and bass stay steady through to about 1:23.
  • About 1:24–1:32: The bass drops out and the track fades.
  • About 1:33–1:43: Silence, while the picture carries on.

In the Nighttime

  • 0:00–0:11: A quiet intro with no measurable sub-bass.
  • About 0:12: The bass arrives and voice starts at about 0:13. There's one short dip near 0:24.
  • About 0:27–1:02: The main body, at a steady level.
  • About 1:03–1:14: The low end thins and the song winds down. The music stops at about 1:14, and the last few seconds of the video are silent.

Side by side

The two songs are built differently. "Nightmares" starts the vocals right away and holds the beat back for almost half a minute. "In the Nighttime" brings the bass in within about 12 seconds and gets to its main section faster. That suits a song that is only about 74 seconds long.

A beat-tracking script gave rough tempo estimates of about 136 BPM for "Nightmares" and about 144 BPM for "In the Nighttime". Treat those as approximate. Automated tempo detection often reads half-time or double-time by mistake, especially with drill's off-beat hi-hats.

The auto-captions picked up roughly 210 words of voice in "Nightmares" and roughly 170 in "In the Nighttime". Neither song shows the large repeated block of text you'd expect from a full chorus. We found only short phrases repeated within a few seconds of each other (near 0:15/0:22 in "Nightmares" and near 0:37/0:39 in "In the Nighttime"). Captions do mishear words, so treat that as an indication, not a final answer.

Where "Nightmares" comes from

Like "4 Minutes of Hell", "Nightmares" first appeared on Different Breed. That's the nine-track project Apple Music lists with a ℗ 2024 str8hitsrecords credit and a February 5, 2024 track date. The album version is 1:34. That fits with the roughly 1:32 of music in the video once you allow for the title card. So the 2026 video is a new visual for a song already released in 2024. We haven't confirmed the video uses the same mix.

We couldn't find "In the Nighttime" in Apple's public catalogue search. It may be listed under a different spelling, out on another platform, or not released for streaming.

On the "drill" label

Both YouTube titles call these songs Toronto drill. Wikipedia describes drill music as a hip-hop subgenre with sonic roots in trap, which started in Chicago in the early 2010s. Drill lyrics are often confrontational. That's true of both of these songs too, and it's part of why we don't reproduce any lines here. This post is about how the videos are made, not about the subject matter of the lyrics.
