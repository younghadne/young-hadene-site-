Young Hadene Interview: On ‘They Took Me Down’, Toronto Drill & the Independent Grind (2026 Q&A)

Editor’s note: this exclusive Q&A is an editorial feature told in Young Hadene’s voice — the stories behind the music, straight from the artist.

With his 11-track album ‘they took me down’ out now and a fresh feature on Exposed Vocals, Young Hadene sat down — pen, not microphone — to answer ten questions about the project, his Haitian-Toronto roots, the independent grind, and where Toronto drill goes from here.

The Album & Its Meaning

Q1: ‘They took me down’ just dropped — 11 tracks. What does this album mean to you?

Young Hadene: Everything. This is the most honest body of work I ever put together. Eleven tracks, no filler, no skips — every song is a chapter. The title is real life: I been counted out, talked about, set up to fail. People really tried to take me down. But I’m still here, still dropping, still independent. When you press play on ‘they took me down’ on Spotify, you’re hearing what survival sounds like.

Q2: The title sounds personal. Who took you down?

Young Hadene: I’m not naming names — the music says what it needs to say. But everybody from the streets knows how it goes: fake love, people switching up when things get heavy, doors closing because you won’t compromise. I turned all of that into fuel. Instead of crashing out, I went to the studio. That’s the difference between me and a lot of artists — my pain becomes product. Every disappointment on this album got turned into a hook.

Q3: How did you decide which songs made the final 11?

Young Hadene: It started as way more than eleven. I probably recorded thirty records for this project. The rule was simple: if a song didn’t make me feel something on the first listen a week later, it got cut. I’d record, sit with it, drive around the city at night listening, and ask — does this move me? The eleven that survived all passed that test. Cohesion mattered more than quantity. It had to play front to back like one story, not a playlist.

Roots, Sound & Identity

Q4: Born in Haiti, raised in Toronto. How do both worlds show up in the music?

Young Hadene: Haiti is in my blood and Toronto raised me, so the music carries both. You’ll hear Caribbean cadence in how I ride a beat — the bounce, the pockets I choose, certain melodies that come from island music. Then the content is straight 6ix: the pressure, the ambition, the loyalty codes of the streets here. I’m Haitian-Toronto, and I don’t water either side down. That fusion is my signature. Nobody else in the city sounds like me because nobody else lived my exact story.

Q5: Describe your sound for someone who’s never heard you before.

Young Hadene: Dark trap meets Toronto drill with melody. Heavy 808s that shake the car, haunting beats, and I switch cadences mid-verse — I might start aggressive then slide into something almost sung. I call my whole aesthetic Trap Blueprints and Hood Rich Stories: real street narratives with emotional depth. Start with “No Sleep” and “6ix Side,” then go run the new album. You’ll get it within three songs.

Independence & Press

Q6: You’re fully independent under Str8hitsRecords. What’s the hardest part of that grind?

Young Hadene: Doing everything. I’m the artist, the A&R, the marketing department, the budget. Nobody’s handing me a rollout — I build it. The hardest part is patience: watching artists with machine backing blow past while you’re funding your own videos and pitching your own records. But the flip side is ownership. I own my masters, my vision, my timeline. Nobody can shelve me. Every stream on my Spotify page comes directly to the team that made it. That’s worth more than a fast advance.

Q7: Exposed Vocals just featured the album in their Press Room. What did that mean to you?

Young Hadene: Major love for that. I actually wrote about it — here’s my full thank-you to Exposed Vocals — because platforms that cover independent artists for real deserve their flowers. Their feature on ‘they took me down’ gave the album a permanent press home with streaming links and everything. For an indie artist, that kind of coverage travels — curators find you through it, bloggers reference it. One link becomes ten opportunities. So yeah, salute to them.

Toronto Drill & What’s Next

Q8: Where is Toronto drill headed right now, in your view?

Young Hadene: It’s getting more melodic and more global at the same time. The pure aggression era evolved — now you need hooks, you need atmosphere, you need something that works outside the block. And the Caribbean and African influences are taking over the sound of the city, whether people admit it or not. Toronto drill in 2026 is really world music with 808s. The artists who understand that — who bring their heritage into the production instead of copying Chicago for the hundredth time — those are the ones who’ll last.

Q9: Take us inside your studio process. How does a Young Hadene record get made?

Young Hadene: Late nights. Most of my best work happens after midnight when the city’s quiet — that’s literally where “No Sleep” came from. I don’t really write on paper much anymore; I catch melodies first, mumble flows until the pocket locks, then the words come. The beat has to be dark — minor keys, space in the production so my voice is the main instrument. Then ad-libs last, mixed like instrumentation. If the hook doesn’t stick in my head by morning, we rework it. Simple as that.

Q10: What’s next — and what’s your message to the fans, to Z Nation?

Young Hadene: Visuals for the album, more shows, and I’m already working — I never stop. To Z Nation: thank you for streaming, for sharing, for showing up. We built this with no machine, just consistency and real music. Go run up ‘they took me down’, send it to one person who never heard me, and watch what we do next. We’re just getting started. The ones who tried to take me down are about to watch me go up.
