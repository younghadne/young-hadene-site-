How to Make Toronto Drill Beats (2026)

Toronto drill beats have a sound you can pick out in a few seconds: dark melodies, sliding 808s, off-beat snares and a lot of space. This guide walks through how to make a Toronto drill beat from scratch, what goes into a good drum kit, where rage beats fit in, and how to find (or offer) free Toronto type beats without getting burned on licensing.

The anatomy of a Toronto drill beat

Tempo: around 140 to 145 BPM, programmed in half-time so it feels slower.

Key: almost always minor. Natural minor and harmonic minor are both common; harmonic minor gives you that eerie raised seventh.

Elements: 1. Melody (piano, strings, choir, bells, flute, guitar or a dark pad) 2. Sliding 808 3. Kick (sometimes none; the 808 can carry the low end) 4. Snare and/or clap, syncopated 5. Hi-hats with triplet rolls 6. Percussion and FX (rims, shakers, risers, reverse sounds)

Step 1: The melody

Start with a simple loop, two or four bars. Toronto drill melodies tend to be sparse and cold. Fewer notes, more mood. A few approaches:

  • A slow piano line in a minor key with a lot of reverb
  • Strings or a choir holding long chords under a short counter-melody
  • A sample (make sure you can clear it or use a royalty-free source)

Keep the melody out of the low end. That space belongs to the 808.

Step 2: The drums

Drill drums are what separate the genre from trap.

Snares: in trap, the snare lands steadily on beat 3 of each bar in half-time. In drill it moves. A common starting pattern puts snares on the fourth beat and then adds an off-beat hit late in the bar, so the groove skips. Try moving a snare a sixteenth note early or late until the beat starts to bounce.

Hi-hats: program a steady pattern, then add triplet rolls and short gaps. Change the velocity of individual hits so it doesn't sound robotic. Small pitch changes on rolls help.

Kick: keep it simple. Many drill beats use the 808 itself as the kick. If you do use a kick, tune it to the 808 so they don't clash.

Percussion: rim shots, shakers, and short percussion hits fill gaps between snares without cluttering the groove.

Step 3: The 808

The 808 is the signature of drill. Program it to follow or answer the melody, and use glide on overlapping notes to create slides. Slides between the root and the fifth, or up an octave, are classic moves.

  • Tune the 808 to the song's key.
  • Use short slides often. That's what gives drill its motion.
  • Leave silence before a big slide so it lands.
  • Add light saturation so it can be heard on phones and earbuds.

For 808 mixing details, see the section in Dark Trap vs Drill: Sound, Songwriting & 808s.

Step 4: Arrangement

A beat for a rapper needs room for a song. A simple structure:

  • Intro (4 to 8 bars): melody only, or melody with filtered drums
  • Hook (8 bars): full beat
  • Verse (16 bars): full beat, maybe with a slightly thinner melody
  • Hook (8 bars)
  • Verse (16 bars)
  • Hook (8 bars)
  • Outro

Drops, where the drums cut out for a beat or a bar, give the artist moments to land a line.

Building a drill drum kit

You don't need to buy dozens of kits. You need a small set of sounds that work together:

  • Two or three 808s with different characters (clean, distorted, long tail)
  • A few snares and claps that cut through a dark mix
  • Hi-hats that are crisp but not harsh
  • Rims and percussion
  • A handful of FX: risers, reverse cymbals, vinyl noise, gunshot-style or impact sounds used sparingly

Before you use any kit, read the licence. Some free kits are royalty-free for any use. Others are free only for non-commercial use. Organise your sounds into folders so you're not scrolling through hundreds of files mid-session.

Hard drill beats: making it hit

If you want a beat to sound harder:

  • Strip the melody back. A colder, simpler loop often feels heavier.
  • Push the 808. More saturation, more slides, more rhythm.
  • Use darker sounds: low strings, choirs, detuned bells.
  • Make the snare pattern more aggressive with extra off-beat hits.
  • Leave space for the vocal. A beat that's busy everywhere actually feels weaker.

Rage beats and Toronto rap

Rage beats came out of the US: loud, distorted synth leads, fast energy and heavy 808s, built for mosh pits. Some Toronto artists mix rage elements with drill and dark trap, using a distorted synth lead over drill drums or a dark trap 808. If you want to try it, keep the drill groove and swap the cold piano for a buzzy, saturated synth. Treat it as a flavour, not a replacement.

Free Toronto type beats: what rappers should know

"Free" beats on YouTube are often free to download but not free to release. Read the description:

  • Free for non-profit: you can use it for a freestyle or demo, not for a streaming release.
  • Free with credit: you can release it if you credit the producer exactly as asked.
  • Leasing: a paid licence with limits on streams or sales.
  • Exclusive: you buy the beat outright; the producer stops selling it.

If you plan to release a song, get the terms in writing. A song pulled from streaming because of a beat dispute is a waste of everything you put into it.

Making beats for artists

If you want artists to use your beats:

  • Tag your beats so they can't be released without permission, but keep the tag light.
  • Make beats with space for a voice.
  • Name your files clearly with tempo and key.
  • Reach out to artists whose sound actually fits your beats, not everyone at once.

FAQ

What BPM are Toronto drill beats? Usually around 140 to 145 BPM, felt in half-time.

What DAW is best for drill? Any of them. FL Studio is popular with drill producers, but Ableton Live, Logic Pro and others all work.

Can I release a song on a free type beat? Only if the licence allows it. Read the terms and get them in writing.
