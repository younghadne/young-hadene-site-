How to Write Drill Lyrics & Hooks (Toronto Style)

Good drill lyrics sound effortless, but they're built carefully: tight cadences, specific details, a voice nobody else has, and a hook that sticks after one listen. This guide covers how to write drill lyrics in a Toronto style, how to tell stories in street rap, how to write melodic drill hooks that stick, and how to study the city's best lyricists without copying them.

Start with what's true

The most important rule in drill is the simplest: write what you know. Drill fans, and Toronto fans especially, can tell when an artist is borrowing a life that isn't theirs. Your neighbourhood, your people, your pressure, your goals: that's your material. You don't need to exaggerate it. Specific and honest beats loud and generic every time.

Know the beat before you write

Drill lyrics are written to the drums. Before you write a line, loop the beat and listen:

  • Where do the snares land? Drill snares are syncopated, so your syllables should play around them.
  • Where are the gaps? Silence before an 808 slide is a perfect place to land a punchline.
  • How fast is it? At around 140 BPM in half-time, you can ride it slow and heavy or double up.

Mumble or hum a flow over the beat first. Find the rhythm, then put words to it. For how the beats are built, see How to Make Toronto Drill Beats.

Cadence: the core of drill

Drill is cadence-first. A few techniques:

Switch it up. Change your flow every four or eight bars. Go from a steady flow to a stop-start one, or from straight rhythm to triplets.

Use pauses. Cutting off mid-phrase and letting the beat breathe can hit harder than another line.

Stack internal rhymes. Rhyme within lines, not just at the end. It keeps the flow tight.

Lean on the slides. Land key words on 808 slides so the bass and the voice hit together.

Storytelling in street rap

The best street rap tells a story, even in a short verse. A simple structure:

  1. Set the scene. Where are we? Time of day, place, who's there. One or two concrete details.
  2. Introduce the tension. What's at stake? Who's a threat? What's the pressure?
  3. Turn. Something happens: a decision, a betrayal, a win, a loss.
  4. Land it. How does it end, and what did it mean?

Show, don't explain. "Mom's on the late shift, fridge looking empty" says more than "we were poor."

Use specific details. Street names, objects, times, sounds. Details make it real.

Have a point of view. Is this a warning? A memory? A brag? A confession? Pick one per song.

Themes that run through drill

Loyalty, betrayal, pressure, money, survival, ambition, grief and growth come up again and again. That's not a weakness; it's the shared language of the genre. Your job is to say something about those themes that only you could say. Young Hadene, a Haitian-born, Toronto-based artist on Str8hitsRecords, titled one of his albums Sacrifices & Success, a name that points straight at two of those themes.

Slang: use your own

Toronto drill slang leans heavily on Jamaican patois and other Caribbean languages, plus local terms. Use slang that you and your people actually say. Forced slang is one of the fastest ways to lose credibility. If you speak another language at home, consider bringing it in. See Haitian Rappers in Toronto: Creole, Caribbean & Drill for how Creole and other languages work in rap.

How to write melodic drill hooks that stick

A lot of modern drill blends rapped verses with melodic hooks. That contrast, aggressive verse and singable hook, is one of the most effective moves in the genre, and it's central to how drill and dark trap mix. See Dark Trap vs Drill.

Keep it short. Two to four lines. If it's hard to remember, it's too long.

Repeat. Repetition is how hooks get stuck. Repeat a phrase, a melody or both.

Find the melody first. Hum over the beat until a melody feels natural, then write words that fit its shape.

Use simple words. Hooks need to be easy to sing back. Save the complex lines for the verses.

Make it the thesis. The hook should sum up what the song is about.

Contrast with the verse. If the verses are fast and aggressive, make the hook slower and more open.

Studying the greats without copying

Toronto has produced some of the best-known writers in rap, and listening closely to strong lyricists is one of the best ways to improve. When you study a verse you love:

  • Write it out and mark the rhymes, including internal ones.
  • Notice where the flow changes and why.
  • Look at how many specific details they use.
  • Ask what makes the voice recognizable.

Then apply the techniques to your own story. Don't lift lines, flows or concepts. Learn the method, not the words.

Editing

First drafts are rarely the best. When you edit:

  • Cut lines that only exist to rhyme.
  • Replace general words with specific ones.
  • Read it out loud over the beat. If you stumble, fix it.
  • Record a rough take and listen the next day.

Record and refine

Once you've written it, record a demo. A verse can look strong on paper and fall flat on the mic. Adjust the flow, cut what drags, and make sure the hook lands.

FAQ

How many bars is a drill verse? Usually 16, but 8, 12 or 24 are common too. Do what serves the song.

Should I write before or after hearing the beat? After, or at least while it loops. Drill lyrics are built around the drums.

Do I need to use slang? Only the slang you actually use. Honesty matters more than vocabulary.
