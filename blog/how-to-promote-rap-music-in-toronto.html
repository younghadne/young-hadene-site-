Promote Rap Music in Toronto: 2026 Playbook

Making a good song is only half the job. If you're an independent rapper in Toronto, getting people to hear it is the other half, and nobody is going to do it for you. This playbook covers the practical steps: distribution in Canada, the platforms that matter, playlists and Spotify editorial pitching, YouTube and TikTok, blogs and press, labels and demos, and building a fanbase from zero.

Step 1: Get your foundation right

Before you promote anything:

  • Pick a name and stick to it. Make it searchable and consistent across every platform.
  • Claim your profiles. Spotify for Artists, Apple Music for Artists, YouTube (an Official Artist Channel once you qualify), Audiomack and SoundCloud.
  • Get a simple website. One place that links to everything: music, socials, bio, press, contact.
  • Write a short bio. Who you are, where you're from, what you sound like. Keep it factual.
  • Register your songs. In Canada, join SOCAN so you get paid performance royalties as a songwriter.

Step 2: Distribution in Canada

To get on Spotify, Apple Music and other stores, you need a distributor. Popular options for independent artists include DistroKid, TuneCore, CD Baby and others. Compare fees, how royalties are split, and what extras they offer. Read the terms before you sign up.

Upload at least three to four weeks before release. That gives you time to pitch to playlists and set up pre-saves.

Canadian artists can also look into FACTOR, which offers funding programs for recording and marketing. Check the current eligibility rules on their site.

Step 3: Release consistently

Streaming rewards consistency. Rather than one big project a year with silence in between, many independent artists release singles regularly and build toward an album. Each release is a new chance to be found.

Step 4: Playlists and Spotify editorial

Spotify editorial: pitch your unreleased song through Spotify for Artists at least seven days before release (earlier is better). Fill in genre, mood, instruments and your story honestly. Mention that you're Toronto-based and what scene you're part of. There's no guarantee, but it costs nothing.

Apple Music: use Apple Music for Artists and your distributor's pitching options where available.

Independent curators: search for playlists like "Toronto drill," "Canadian drill" and "underground hip-hop Canada." Look for curators who update regularly and list contact info. Some use submission platforms. Pitch songs that actually fit the playlist.

Avoid paid streams and bots. Paying for fake streams or playlist placement can get your music removed. Stick to legitimate curators.

To keep tabs on the scene, read Latest Toronto Drill Songs & Releases.

Step 5: The platforms that matter

YouTube: in drill, YouTube often matters more than audio streaming. Upload official audio, visualizers and music videos. Use clear titles with your name and the song title.

SoundCloud: still a home for underground rap discovery. Good for early versions, freestyles and building a core audience.

Audiomack: popular for hip-hop. Upload your music and use their creator tools.

Apple Music, Spotify and others: these are where most casual listening happens, so make sure your profiles are complete with photos, bio and links.

Step 6: Visuals

Drill is a visual genre. You don't need a big budget:

  • A simple performance video in a real location
  • A visualizer or lyric video
  • Short behind-the-scenes clips

Consistent visuals, including cover art, colours and style, make you recognizable.

Step 7: TikTok, Reels and Shorts

Short-form video is one of the main ways songs get discovered now.

  • Post often. Clips are cheap to make.
  • Lead with the best part of the song in the first two seconds.
  • Show yourself: studio clips, performances, behind the scenes.
  • Use your own song as the sound so people can find it.

Step 8: Blogs, press and YouTube channels

Coverage builds credibility and gives you something to show curators and promoters.

  • Research music blogs and YouTube channels that cover Toronto and Canadian hip-hop.
  • Read their submission guidelines and follow them.
  • Write a short, personal pitch: who you are, one sentence on the song, a streaming link, and a press photo.
  • Don't mass-email. Target outlets that actually cover your style.

Be careful with paid "features" that are really advertisements. They rarely build a real audience. A genuine interview or review is worth more. For example, Young Hadene's album They Took Me Down was covered by Exposed Vocals; see the press page.

Step 9: Labels and demos

Many labels, especially major ones, don't accept unsolicited demos. Smaller independent labels sometimes do. If you approach a label:

  • Build momentum first. Labels look for artists who are already growing.
  • Research the label and its roster.
  • Read any contract carefully and get advice before signing.

Going independent is a real option too. Young Hadene releases independently through Str8hitsRecords, keeping control of his releases.

Step 10: Build a fanbase from zero

  • Talk to your listeners. Reply to comments and messages.
  • Play shows. Open mics and small shows build real fans. See Toronto Hip-Hop Shows, Venues & Open Mics.
  • Collaborate. Work with other artists, producers and videographers.
  • Collect contacts. An email list or community channel you control is worth more than followers on a platform you don't.

Step 11: Measure

Use Spotify for Artists, Apple Music for Artists and YouTube Studio to see where listeners come from, which songs connect, and which cities are listening. Do more of what works.

FAQ

How do I get my music on Spotify in Canada? Use a distributor such as DistroKid, TuneCore or CD Baby.

How early should I pitch to Spotify editorial? At least seven days before release through Spotify for Artists. Earlier is better.

Should I pay for playlist placement? No. Paying for streams or placements risks removal. Pitch legitimate curators instead.
