Kardinal Offishall Is Releasing "Firestarter Vol. 2" Independently. Here's Why That Matters in Toronto

One of the artists who helped put Toronto hip-hop on the map is putting out his next album without a label behind it. In a Canadian Press interview published by CityNews on October 24, Kardinal Offishall talks about Firestarter Vol. 2: Everyday, Sometimes…, the long-awaited follow-up to the record that broke him in 2001, and about his decision to release it on his own terms.

This post covers what the interview confirms, and then looks at two ideas in it that matter to anyone making music in this city right now: independence, and the fact that Toronto has never had just one sound. We're writing as fans and as the team behind an independent Toronto artist. Young Hadene has no connection to Kardinal Offishall or to anyone named in the interview, and nothing here should be read as one.

The interview doesn't give a release date, streaming figures or chart expectations, so we won't either.

According to the report, some of the songs come from ideas he has carried for years. "Top 3" is the loud, confident one. The title track, with BJ the Chicago Kid, is about the days when even getting up feels like too much, and the point he makes is that fame and money don't shield anyone from that.

Beyond the album, the interview mentions an EP in progress with Toronto producer Slakah the Beatchild and a possible collaboration with Canadian R&B singer Glenn Lewis. He also holds two very public roles: judge on Canada's Got Talent and global A&R lead at Def Jam Records.

A record shaped by loss

The album took more than a decade partly because of grief. The interview describes a succession of deaths among people close to him in recent years that left him in no state to make music, and he mentions his late father among the subjects he knew he'd be asked about. It was only after close to a year without another funeral that he found his way back to enjoying the work. He also describes himself as a private person, and says that putting family and loss into songs meant accepting that strangers would ask him about those things.

That's his story to tell, and the interview is the place to read it.

The case for going independent

The part of the interview that stood out most to us is his reasoning for releasing the album himself. He points to streaming and selling directly to fans as the things that give artists room to decide their own path, without needing a record to make a big commercial splash right away. In his words: "It's not as rigid as it used to be in the industry."

That reasoning means more coming from him, because the interview also covers what the label system looked like the first time around. After his 2001 breakthrough, his US label was folded into another company, a planned follow-up fell apart, and he says new executives wanted to fit him into a mould that wasn't his. Money got tight and live work dried up for a while. He eventually went on tour with Akon and Gwen Stefani and spent two months recording in the back of the tour bus. Twenty-five years later, he's choosing a route where those decisions sit with him.

It isn't a story about avoiding labels altogether, though. He's still inside a major, at Def Jam, and the interview says he uses that seat to argue for artists and push back on executives' assumptions. So the real lesson isn't that one path is right. It's that artists now have a real choice, and someone who has seen both sides is choosing independence for his own album.

For independent Toronto artists, a few practical takeaways:

  • Freedom is the main benefit, not a side effect. Without an immediate commercial target, you can release what you actually want to make, including slower, more personal records.
  • Direct-to-fan matters. If the people who listen to you can buy from you and hear you directly, you're less dependent on any one gatekeeper.

Toronto isn't one sound

The other thread worth pulling out is about the idea of a single "Toronto sound." The interview notes that he's on a US press tour that included a stop with a host who once questioned whether the city had a distinctive sound at all. His answer, in short, is that the label boxes artists in. He points to how differently Drake, the Weeknd and Nav sound, and says the thing that ties the city together is quality, not one style. He also passes on a piece of advice he values: lead trends rather than follow them.

The city's history backs that up. His own 2001 album brought the Caribbean-influenced sound of Toronto to a wider audience, and the city has kept adding to that ever since: R&B, dancehall-influenced rap, melodic rap and, more recently, drill and dark trap. They sit side by side.
