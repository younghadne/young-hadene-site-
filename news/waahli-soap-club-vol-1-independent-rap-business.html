Waahli's Soap Club Vol. 1: What a Rapper Who Runs Two Businesses Can Teach Independent Artists

Montréal rapper and singer Waahli has a new project out, Soap Club Vol. 1, and a SOCAN Words & Music feature published on October 23 explains why the title is about more than wordplay. Alongside his music, he runs his own personal care company, and he treats the two as parts of one operation.

This post sums up what the feature confirms about the album and then focuses on the part we found most useful: how an independent artist can build a business around the music instead of relying on the music alone. We're writing as the team behind an independent Toronto artist. Young Hadene has no connection to Waahli or anyone named in the feature, and nothing here suggests otherwise.

About the album

The soap theme runs through the whole project. SOCAN says the title nods to the film Fight Club, in which soap plays a big part, and that Waahli likes the word "club" because it suggests a community. There's also a playful Creole-flavoured take on an OutKast song title, turning "So Fresh, So Clean" into "Tou fre, tout propre."

Musically, the feature describes a mix of pop, rock, dancehall and kompa folded into his hip-hop, with verses that move between English, French and Haitian Creole, sometimes inside one song. He worked with younger producers, but the record really took shape in São Paulo, at the home of an old friend from Nomadic Massive, where he went to step back and reset. A song he wrote there, "Lumière," ended up setting the tone for the rest.

The album opens with "St-Mich," a shout-out to Saint-Michel, the Montréal neighbourhood he grew up in. His parents are from Haiti, and he describes that heritage and that neighbourhood as the base of his identity. We've written about how Haitian heritage shapes rap in this country, and in Toronto in particular, in our piece on Haitian rappers in Toronto, so we won't repeat that here.

A slow build, then a breakthrough

One detail in the feature is easy to skim past: Waahli was named a Révélation Radio-Canada artist in 2023, close to 20 years after he first appeared with Nomadic Massive. He describes himself as both a veteran and a newcomer at once, with long-time listeners and new ones arriving together.

That's worth sitting with if you're an independent artist. Recognition doesn't follow a schedule. A group career and a solo debut came long before the moment that brought him a wider audience.

The rapper-founder model

Here's the part we think matters most. Waahli runs Wyzah Musk, a personal care company, and he doesn't treat it as a separate hobby. He describes the music and the company as one connected system, telling SOCAN: "It's all part of the same ecosystem." Money earned in one side can go back into the other, and the company gives him merch that goes beyond the standard T-shirt.

Look at how tightly the pieces fit. His solo debut was called Black Soap. The new project is Soap Club Vol. 1. He sells personal care products. A fan who likes the album has a natural next step that isn't another shirt, and the brand and the music keep pointing back at each other. That's not a gimmick bolted on afterwards. The theme was there from his first solo release.

We don't know Wyzah Musk's revenue, product range or how the money splits between the two sides, because the feature doesn't say. But the model itself is clear, and it offers a few lessons that apply well beyond Montréal.

Diversify, but connect. Plenty of artists have side income. The difference here is that the second business reinforces the art instead of distracting from it. If you start something alongside your music, it works best when fans can see the link.

Reinvest on purpose. Moving money between two ventures means a slow month in one doesn't stop the other. For an independent artist with no label advance, that kind of internal funding can pay for a video, a mix or a run of shows.

Make merch mean something. A product tied to the concept of your work tells a story a generic shirt can't. It doesn't have to be soap. The point is that the item and the music share an idea.

Build a community, not just a fanbase. Waahli's own reading of the word "club" is about belonging. Independent artists rarely win on reach alone. People sticking around because they feel part of something is often what keeps a career going.

Step away when you need to. The album came together after he left home to reset. Running a business and a music career at once is a lot, and the feature suggests that distance is what let the record take shape.

What this means for independent artists in Toronto

Young Hadene is an independent artist with Haitian roots, based in Toronto, making drill and dark trap. His most recent full-length is the 11-track album They Took Me Down, released October 2, 2026. We're not comparing his path to Waahli's, and the styles are very different. But the question the feature raises is one every independent artist faces: how do you fund the work and keep control of it at the same time?

Waahli's answer is to own more than one piece of the operation and let them support each other. It's not the only answer, but it's a concrete one, and it's built on choices an independent artist can actually make.
